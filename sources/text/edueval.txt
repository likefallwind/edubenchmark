EduEval: A Hierarchical Cognitive Benchmark for Evaluating Large
Language Models in Chinese Education

Abstract

arXiv:2512.00290v1 [cs.CL] 29 Nov 2025

Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate
significant potential for educational applications. However, their unscrutinized deployment
poses risks to educational standards, underscoring the need for rigorous evaluation. We introduce EduEval, a comprehensive hierarchical
benchmark for evaluating LLMs in Chinese K12 education. This benchmark makes three key
contributions: (1) Cognitive Framework: We
propose the EduAbility Taxonomy, which unifies Bloom’s Taxonomy and Webb’s Depth of
Knowledge to organize tasks across six cognitive dimensions including Memorization, Understanding, Application, Reasoning, Creativity, and Ethics. (2) Authenticity: Our benchmark integrates real exam questions, classroom conversation, student essays, and expertdesigned prompts to reflect genuine educational challenges; (3) Scale: EduEval comprises 24 distinct task types with over 11,000
questions spanning primary to high school levels. We evaluate 14 leading LLMs under both
zero-shot and few-shot settings, revealing that
while models perform well on factual tasks,
they struggle with classroom dialogue classification and exhibit inconsistent results in creative content generation. Interestingly, several open source models outperform proprietary systems on complex educational reasoning. Few-shot prompting shows varying effectiveness across cognitive dimensions, suggesting that different educational objectives
require tailored approaches. These findings
provide targeted benchmarking metrics for developing LLMs specifically optimized for diverse Chinese educational tasks. The EduEval dataset and code are publicly available at
https://github.com/Maerzs/E_edueval.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language models
(LLMs) in natural language processing (NLP) and
artificial intelligence (AI) has opened new possibilities for their application in education. With strong

capabilities in language understanding, reasoning, and text generation, LLMs have been widely
adopted in intelligent tutoring, automated grading,
and educational content generation, significantly
enhancing personalized learning and instructional
efficiency (Brown et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022;
Wei et al., 2022). Their impact now extends from
primary and secondary education to higher education and vocational training (Glaser et al., 2001;
Kasneci et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2023), with great
potential shown in real world teaching activities
like lesson preparation assistance and interactive
classroom support (Kasneci et al., 2023; Chang
et al., 2024; Gan et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025).
However, evaluating LLMs in authentic educational settings remains a significant challenge
(Guo et al., 2021). The educational process is inherently dynamic and heterogeneous (Koopmans,
2020), with diverse student cognitive profiles requiring constant adaptation (Haelermans, 2022).
Current mainstream evaluation methods rely heavily on static assessments (Chen et al., 2024), such
as standardized multiple choice questions, which
fail to capture the complex reasoning, generative
expression, and ethical decision making required in
real world classrooms (Allen and Kendeou, 2024;
Yan et al., 2024). As a result, these evaluations tend
to underestimate the true educational potential of
LLMs (Srivastava et al., 2022). While general purpose benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks et al.,
2020), BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022), and
HELM (Liang et al., 2022) cover broad abilities,
they fall short in addressing the specific needs of
educational applications. Similarly, Chinese benchmarks like E-Eval (Hou et al., 2024), AGIEval
(Zhong et al., 2024), and C-Eval (Huang et al.,
2023) are still dominated by low level cognitive
questions and single-turn formats, lacking systematic evaluation of higher-order abilities and providing limited diversity in task contexts and cognitive
depth (Long et al., 2024).

To bridge this gap, a robust benchmark must assess a spectrum of cognitive abilities grounded in
authentic pedagogical contexts. Established frameworks like Bloom’s Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (Krathwohl, 2002) and Webb’s Depth of
Knowledge (DOK) model (Masharipova, 2024)
provide a foundation for structuring cognitive levels, but a comprehensive evaluation requires a synthesized framework tailored specifically for the
multifaceted demands of K-12 education. We introduce EduEval, a hierarchical benchmark designed to meet this need by systematically evaluating LLMs on Chinese K-12 educational tasks.
EduEval prioritizes educational authenticity by
incorporating tasks directly from teaching practice.
Unlike traditional benchmarks, it includes classroom dialogue classification, student essay scoring,
and teaching scenario design activities that reflect
educators’ daily challenges. To ensure a comprehensive and authentic assessment, we developed
a curriculum-balanced dataset spanning core subjects across developmental stages from primary to
high school. All tasks derive from genuine educational sources, including examination archives,
classroom transcripts, and expert-created materials.
Our rigorous quality control employs a two-phase
annotation protocol where practicing educators provide initial assessments before domain experts conduct thorough reviews, ensuring alignment with
actual pedagogical standards rather than artificial
benchmarking metrics.
Our systematic evaluation of mainstream language models (including both general-purpose and
education-specific systems) reveals a consistent
performance gradient across cognitive dimensions.
Models generally excel at knowledge-oriented
tasks, with top performers achieving over 90% accuracy in areas like formula recall, but struggle
significantly with contextual application in authentic educational scenarios, where accuracy can drop
below 30% for tasks such as classroom dialogue
classification. This pattern mirrors the complexity
progression in human learning and highlights key
areas for model improvement. By identifying these
capability patterns across the EduAbility hierarchy, our benchmark provides not merely a scoring
system but a developmental blueprint for creating
AI systems that can meaningfully support the full
spectrum of educational processes.
In summary, we make three key contributions:
1) We propose the EduAbility Taxonomy, a novel
and hierarchical cognitive framework that inte-

grates Bloom’s Taxonomy and Webb’s Depth of
Knowledge to systematically organize educational
evaluation tasks across six cognitive dimensions.
2) We establish EduEval, a comprehensive and
authentic benchmark for Chinese K-12 education, featuring over 11,000 questions across 24 task
types derived from genuine classroom scenarios,
including dialogue classification and essay scoring.
3) We present a holistic evaluation of 14 leading LLMs on this benchmark, providing a detailed
analysis of their educational capabilities and a developmental blueprint for creating more effective
AI systems for education.

Related Work

In recent years, large language models (LLMs)
have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across
various domains through mechanisms such as pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and alignment
with human or AI feedback (Bai et al., 2022; Christiano et al., 2017; Dong et al., 2023, 2024). With
the continuous advancement of natural language
processing technology, benchmarks have expanded
to encompass more complex tasks, including machine translation (Bojar et al., 2014) and summarization (Narayan et al., 2018; Hermann et al.,
2015). Comprehensive benchmarks like GLUE
(Wang et al., 2019) and SuperGLUE (Sarlin et al.,
2020) amalgamate various natural language understanding tasks, providing standards for evaluating model performance. Popular frameworks
like MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020) include tasks
ranging from real-world exams to book knowledge,
assessing ability in language understanding, common sense reasoning (Bhakthavatsalam et al., 2021;
Bian et al., 2021; Sakaguchi et al., 2021), and code
generation (Chen et al., 2021; Austin et al., 2021).
With the burgeoning development of Chinese
Large Models, an increasing number of Chinese
benchmarks have emerged. CLUE (Xu et al., 2020),
an influential Chinese NLU benchmark, has been
widely adopted. In addition, Chinese benchmarks
similar to MMLU have surfaced, such as MMCU
(Zeng, 2023), which encompasses medicine and
education within its four main domains. AGIEval
(Zhong et al., 2024) focuses on standardized Chinese exams, while C-Eval (Huang et al., 2023) includes 13,948 questions on four levels of difficulty,
from middle school to professional tests. M3KE
(Liu et al., 2023) gathers 71 tasks from the Chinese
education examination system, akin to the coverage

EduEval actualizes this logical progression

EduEval

3.1

Design Principles

Traditional cognitive taxonomies present limitations when applied to comprehensive LLM evaluation in educational contexts. Bloom’s Taxonomy (Krathwohl, 2002), while influential, assumes
rigid hierarchical progression and lacks consideration of concurrent cognitive processes. Webb’s
Depth of Knowledge (DOK) model (Masharipova,
2024) addresses cognitive complexity but inadequately captures the multidimensional nature of educational cognition. Neither framework sufficiently
addresses ethical reasoning, which is crucial for educational AI systems. We propose the EduAbility
Taxonomy to address these limitations by integrating both frameworks’ strengths while introducing
novel dimensions. Our approach maps Memory
and Understanding levels to DOK Levels 1-2 and

Bloom’s foundational skills, Application and Reasoning levels to DOK Level 3 with Bloom’s analytical capabilities, and Creativity level to Bloom’s
highest-order thinking and DOK Level 4 demands.
Additionally, we introduce an Ethics dimension to
address moral reasoning in educational contexts.
As depicted in Figure 1, the EduAbility Taxonomy organizes tasks into six hierarchical cognitive levels: Memory, Understanding, Application,
Reasoning, Creativity, and Ethics. These levels
form an interconnected cognitive scaffold where
higher-level tasks build on foundational abilities
while operating synergistically. Memory tasks assess recall of core concepts. Understanding requires interpretation and explanation. Application
involves real world problem-solving (e.g., Essay
Scoring, Classroom Dialogue Classification), Reasoning evaluates causal analysis and complex inference. Creativity encompasses innovative generation and knowledge integration, and Ethics addresses moral judgment in educational contexts.
The theoretical foundations, detailed framework
mappings, and empirical validation of this taxonomy are extensively discussed in the Appendix B.
Each level includes several specific evaluation
tasks that correspond to the respective abilities. For
example, our Classroom Dialogue Classification
tasks utilize real teacher-student interactions, while
our Essay Scoring task requires evaluating student
compositions according to standard educational
rubrics. It is essential to note that this taxonomy
does not imply a linear progression of abilities:
the cognitive dimensions are interdependent, with
higher-level reasoning often requiring strong foun-

3.2

Data Collection and Processing

Data Source. EduEval is constructed from a
blend of authentic educational content and expertauthored materials to comprehensively evaluate
language models across six cognitive levels. For
memory, understanding, reasoning, and application
tasks, we collect standardized exam questions from
Gaokao, Zhongkao, and various primary and middle school assessments, covering core subjects such
as Chinese, Mathematics, English, and Science.
The essay scoring task is based on real studentwritten essays collected from diverse school contexts, each paired with ground-truth scores assigned by certified instructors using official grading
rubrics. Classroom dialogue classification tasks are
built from transcripts of real classroom interactions,
which are annotated by experienced educators according to a predefined taxonomy of pedagogical

discourse types. Creative tasks and ethical reasoning tasks are entirely designed by domain experts.
While creative tasks such as teaching design or
question generation require open-ended model responses, ethical tasks simulate realistic dilemmas
related to fairness, bias, or privacy in school settings, and are presented in structured formats with
predefined choices. These tasks are intended to
probe the model’s moral and normative alignment,
without relying on externally annotated labels. A
detailed mapping between task types and their corresponding data sources is provided in Appendix C.
Data Processing. To operationalize the EduAbility cognitive scaffold on authentic and often
unstructured educational materials, a methodological architecture of commensurate sophistication is
required. Consequently, we designed and implemented a multi-agent processing pipeline whose
structure is intentionally engineered to mirror the
interconnected nature of the cognitive framework
itself. This architecture serves the dual purpose
of ensuring high-fidelity conversion of diverse raw
materials (PDFs, Word documents, web content)
into a unified JSON schema, while systematically
embedding our theoretical principles into the data
curation process. The pipeline includes: (1) Format
Conversion Agent for OCR extraction and mathematical notation processing; (2) Standardization
Agent for schema validation and field extraction;
(3) Task Format Optimizer that renders objective
tasks as multiple-choice and preserves free-form

structure for open ended tasks; (4) Quality Control
Agent for duplicate detection and anomaly flagging. This architecture enables scalable processing
of 11,150+ items while maintaining high fidelity.
Details in Appendix C.1.
Data Annotation. The annotation process itself
is a hybrid human-AI framework, engineered to
embed our theoretical principles directly into the
dataset. A Task Routing Agent first assigns items
to appropriate annotation tracks based on task type,
after which Annotation Support Agents provide
draft suggestions that human experts can accept
or override. All subjective labels then undergo a
rigorous double-blind cross-review, managed by a
Consensus Orchestration Agent, to achieve an interannotator agreement threshold of over 90%. Crucially, a Cognitive Alignment Verification Agent
operationalizes our theoretical framework, using
a fine-tuned classifier to validate that each item
is rigorously mapped to its primary dimension
within the EduAbility cognitive scaffold. This
multi-agent, human-in-the-loop approach ensures
that each benchmark item is not only internally consistent but also theoretically grounded, combining
the irreplaceable judgment of educational experts
with the systematic rigor of AI-driven validation.
Complete protocols in Appendix E.
3.3

Task Definitions

To evaluate LLMs across varied cognitive dimensions, we construct tasks grounded in the EduAbility Taxonomy, covering memory, understanding,
application, reasoning, creativity, and ethics. Memory tasks assess factual recall, such as mathematical formulas and historical facts. Understanding
tasks test conceptual interpretation, including reading comprehension and poetry analysis. Application tasks reflect real-world classroom scenarios
like essay scoring and dialogue classification. Reasoning tasks involve logical inference and crossdisciplinary integration. Creativity tasks require
open-ended generation, such as teaching design
and question creation. Ethics tasks present dilemmas on fairness and moral judgment in education.
A summary of task categories is shown in Table 1,
with further details and examples in Appendix C.3.

4

Evaluation

4.1

Experimental Setup

We evaluate all LLMs in both zero-shot and fewshot settings. In the zero-shot setting, each input

consists of a task instruction and query, while in the
few-shot setting, we provide three illustrative examples per task. To ensure consistency, all prompts
are presented in Chinese, and for chat-based models, we preserve their native dialogue format. We
set the decoding temperature to 0 during inference
to reduce randomness in model outputs. When
an input exceeds the model’s maximum context
length, we truncate it from the middle to preserve
instruction and answer segments. Multiple-choice
tasks are evaluated using Accuracy, and generative
tasks are evaluated using Rouge-L or GPT-based
alignment metrics. The evaluation metrics used for
each task are listed in Table 1, and prompt formats
are provided in Appendix C.4. For experimental
hardware configuration, refer to Appendix D.
4.2

Evaluated Models

We evaluated 14 large language models varying in
size, organization, and design objective, as summarized in Table 11. For commercial models,
we included GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024), Qwen14B-Chat (Bai et al., 2023), GLM4-9B-Chat (Zeng
et al., 2023), and Spark-X1 (iFLYTEK Research,
2024), representing leading proprietary systems.
Among open-source systems, we assessed Qwen72B and Qwen-7B (Bai et al., 2023), Yi-34B-Chat
and Yi-6B-Chat (01.AI, 2023), Chinese-LLaMA213B (Cui et al., 2023), DeepSeek-R1-32B (Team,
2024), LLaMA3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and
Baichuan-13B (Yang et al., 2023). These models
span a wide range of parameter scales and alignment strategies, enabling controlled comparisons
across reasoning, generation, and ethical tasks. To
further explore domain-specific capabilities, we included EduChat-sft-002-13B-Baichuan (Dan et al.,
2023), which is fine-tuned from Baichuan-13B
specifically for educational tasks, allowing for a direct analysis of the impact of domain specialization.
The relevant model details are in the Appendix D.

Figure 2 illustrates the performance of the top
six models across six educational cognitive ability
dimensions. From the results, we can derive several important observations. Models demonstrate
significantly stronger capabilities in Memory tasks
compared to Application tasks, revealing a fundamental challenge in translating recalled knowledge
into practical problem-solving scenarios. This gap
suggests that while models can effectively retrieve
educational content, they struggle with the dynamic
application of this knowledge, particularly in classroom discourse classification, where performance
drops substantially across all systems.
Performance in Reasoning and Ethics dimensions exhibits considerable variance across models.
The highest performing systems achieve impressive results in logical reasoning tasks (85-90%),
but even these models show a sharp decline when
confronted with multi-step reasoning problems requiring sustained logical chains. This indicates that
enhancing reasoning architectures through carefully designed pretraining objectives could yield
substantial improvements in educational applications requiring complex problem solving.
In the Creativity dimension, all models demonstrate moderate capabilities in content generation,
yet struggle with more structured tasks like teaching design. This limitation stems primarily from
the inherent tension between creative flexibility and
pedagogical structure. Future development should
focus on incorporating educational-specific templates and exemplars during fine-tuning to balance
creative generation with instructional coherence.

Ethics represents the most consistent dimension across all models, with relatively strong performance even from mid tier systems. However,
this uniformity masks significant variations in handling complex ethical scenarios involving competing values or culturally sensitive educational
contexts, pointing to the need for more nuanced
alignment techniques that incorporate diverse educational value systems.
To address these limitations, we recommend developing specialized educational reasoning frameworks, incorporating curriculum-informed training objectives, and designing evaluation methods
that better reflect authentic educational interactions.
This would transform these systems from knowledge repositories into effective educational tools.

5

Conclusion and Future Work

We presented EduEval, a comprehensive benchmark for LLMs in Chinese K-12 education
grounded in the novel EduAbility Taxonomy. Our
evaluation of 14 leading models revealed a critical gap between strong factual recall and weak
practical application, particularly in authentic scenarios like classroom dialogue classification. Results also showed a surprising performance hierarchy: models like Spark-X1 and Qwen-plus outperformed other leading proprietary systems in complex educational reasoning. Additionally, narrow,
domain-specific fine-tuning may constrain broader
cognitive abilities. These insights validate EduEval as a powerful diagnostic tool and highlight the
urgent need for curriculum-balanced pre-training
and improved reasoning architectures. Looking
ahead, we plan to expand EduEval by adding interactive and multimodal scenarios, developing finergrained metrics for creative tasks, and facilitating education-specific alignment strategies. We
welcome community contributions to enrich this
benchmark and advance AI systems that truly support diverse educational needs.

Limitations
Despite EduEval’s comprehensive assessment
framework, there are several limitations. The
benchmark primarily reflects Chinese educational
contexts, potentially limiting international applicability. Our evaluation metrics for creativity and
ethics tasks rely on GPT-based assessment, which
may not fully capture the nuances of these complex
cognitive dimensions despite high correlation with
human judgments. The current benchmark also
lacks multimodal components, overlooking the increasingly visual and interactive nature of modern
education. Additionally, consistently poor performance across models in classroom dialogue classification points to significant challenges in modeling
authentic teacher-student interactions. Future work
will focus on incorporating multimodal tasks, developing more robust evaluation methods for higher
cognitive dimensions, and expanding cross-cultural
comparisons to enhance the benchmark’s utility for
educational applications of LLMs.

Ethics Statement
The development of the EduEval benchmark was
conducted with a strong commitment to ethical
principles throughout the research process. Our
ethical protocols addressed three primary areas:
(1) the protection of data privacy through rigorous
anonymization of all student and teacher-generated
content; (2) the fair and ethical treatment of the
12 educational experts who participated in the annotation process, including fair compensation and
transparent guidelines; and (3) a thorough consideration of the broader societal impacts of our work,
including potential risks and advocating for the responsible use of the benchmark. A comprehensive
discussion of our legal and ethical considerations
is provided in Appendix A.

Legal and Ethical Considerations.

All data undergo strict anonymization procedures

Broader Impact

The EduEval benchmark has potential for a significant positive impact on educational technology and
AI alignment with human learning processes. By
providing a comprehensive evaluation framework
for LLMs across diverse cognitive dimensions, our
work can guide the development of more educationally relevant AI systems that better support authentic teaching and learning. The hierarchical
approach of our benchmark enables targeted improvements in specific educational competencies
from basic knowledge recall to complex ethical
reasoning.

B

EduAbility Taxonomy: Theoretical
Foundation and Framework

This appendix summarizes the theoretical basis of
the EduAbility Taxonomy and its practical use in
EduEval. Traditional taxonomies offer complementary but incomplete views for educational AI assessment: Bloom’s framework emphasizes the type
of cognitive operation, while DOK emphasizes the
depth and rigor of tasks. In authentic classroom
scenarios and model-based evaluation, cognition
is multidimensional and dynamic, so a single-axis
view (type-only or depth-only) is insufficient. EduAbility therefore integrates both perspectives into
a compact, coherent scheme tailored for evaluating
LLMs in education.
EduAbility defines six interrelated levels—Memory, Understanding, Application,
Reasoning, Creativity, and Ethics—and treats
them as an interconnected scaffold rather than a
strict ladder. Memory and Understanding align
with foundational processes and lower depth
(roughly DOK 1–2, lower-order skills in Bloom).
Application and Reasoning integrate strategy and
multi-step inference (roughly DOK 3, Bloom’s
apply/analyze/evaluate). Creativity corresponds to
extended thinking and original synthesis (roughly
DOK 4, Bloom’s create). Ethics is introduced as an
education-specific dimension for moral judgment,
fairness, privacy, and academic integrity, which
is not explicitly covered by traditional cognitive
taxonomies.
The scaffolded nature of the framework reflects
how real tasks recruit multiple abilities at once.
Higher-level tasks routinely invoke foundational
knowledge; conversely, foundational tasks can be
embedded in richer contexts that require interpretation or ethical judgment. This view supports
precise task design and principled difficulty control while preserving authenticity. It also clarifies
why performance can vary across dimensions and
subjects, and how targeted improvements can be
planned.
For implementation, task assignment follows a
two-step principle: identify the dominant cognitive operation, then determine the required depth.
Secondary processes (e.g., recalling facts within a

creative task) are recorded but do not override the
primary label. Ethics is applied whenever normative reasoning is integral to the correct or preferred
outcome. This procedure promotes consistent classification across subjects and grade levels and facilitates reliable evaluation and analysis.
Finally, EduAbility is intended as a practical, extensible taxonomy. The current mapping and definitions are optimized for Chinese K–12 contexts and
LLM assessment; as data and use cases broaden,
the framework can be refined (e.g., by adding sublevels or clarifying cross-level composites) while
preserving the core integration of “operation type”
and “depth” that underpins its validity and utility.

Task Details

C.1

Data Processing Pipeline

We implement an agent-based pipeline that transforms 12,437 raw educational materials into 11,150
standardized benchmark items through four specialized agents. Each agent addresses a specific
processing challenge while maintaining modularity
and error traceability.
C.1.1

Agent 1: Format Conversion Agent

This agent handles heterogeneous input formats
(PDFs, Word documents, LaTeX, web content) and
performs intelligent OCR routing. Based on content analysis, it automatically selects: Tesseract
5.0 for standard Chinese text (98.3% accuracy),
MathPix API for mathematical notation (95.7%
accuracy), or PaddleOCR for complex layouts.
Documents with OCR confidence below 85% are
flagged for human review. The agent applies postprocessing rules to correct common OCR errors
(e.g., 0/O, l/I/1 disambiguation). Output: 12,201
clean text representations (98.1% retention rate).
C.1.2

Agent 2: Standardization Agent

This agent converts processed text into unified
JSON schema containing: task ID, cognitive level,
subject, grade, prompt, options, answer, explanation, difficulty, and evaluation metric. It performs
three validation checks: (1) required field completeness, (2) encoding normalization to UTF-8,
(3) semantic consistency using sentence-BERT to
detect prompt-answer mismatches (threshold: 0.3
similarity). Items failing validation are routed to expert correction queues. This process caught 67 answer key errors. Output: 12,201 schema-compliant
items (100% pass rate).

C.1.3

Agent 3: Task Format Optimizer

This agent determines optimal presentation format
based on cognitive requirements. For objective
tasks (Memory, Understanding, Application, Reasoning, Ethics), it converts items to standardized 4option multiple-choice format (9,250 items, 83%).
When source materials lack sufficient options, it
generates distractors using rule-based heuristics
derived from documented student misconceptions.
For open-ended tasks (Poetry Appreciation, Reading Comprehension, Essay Scoring, Creativity), it
preserves free-form structure and extracts evaluation rubrics into machine-readable format (1,900
items, 17%). Output: 11,991 optimally formatted
items.
C.1.4

Agent 4: Quality Control Agent

This agent implements four validation mechanisms:
(1) Duplicate detection using MinHash LSH (85%
threshold) removes 210 redundant items; (2) Difficulty calibration tests items with three baseline models (GPT-4o, Qwen-72B, GLM4-9B), flagging items with >95% or <10% accuracy—89
outliers reviewed; (3) Cognitive alignment verification uses fine-tuned RoBERTa-Chinese classifier (91.3% accuracy) to validate EduAbility level
assignments, flagging 91 misclassifications; (4)
Anomaly detection identifies statistical outliers
in length, answer distribution, or content quality,
catching 178 additional issues. Output: 11,150
validated items (93.0% final retention).
C.1.5

Agent Orchestration

A central orchestration system coordinates agent
execution, manages error handling, and routes
flagged items to human reviewers. The review dashboard presents: original content, processing history,
specific quality concerns, and contextual aids (e.g.,
scanned images for OCR errors, taxonomy definitions for cognitive misalignments). Average expert
review time: 4.2 minutes per flagged item (327
items total). All agents maintain processing logs
enabling full traceability from source to final item.

C.3 Task Definition and Construction Process
Following the EduAbility cognitive taxonomy, we
designed a suite of tasks targeting six hierarchical
levels of educational ability: Memory, Understanding, Application, Reasoning, Creativity, and Ethics.
Each task is categorized by its dominant cognitive
level, although some tasks inevitably engage multiple levels of thinking. Our classification reflects
the principal skill emphasized in the prompt and expected response, ensuring consistency across task
design and evaluation.

The task set includes both data-derived and
expert-constructed components. Tasks aligned with
Memory, Understanding, Reasoning, and Application are primarily adapted from real-world educational materials, including national and regional
exams (e.g., Gaokao, Zhongkao), textbook-based
assessments, and authentic classroom transcripts.
In contrast, Creativity and Ethics tasks are fully designed by expert annotators to capture open-ended
generation, pedagogical planning, and moral reasoning in education-specific contexts.
For tasks requiring manual creation or postediting (e.g., classroom dialogue classification, ethical dilemmas, and all creativity tasks), we provide a high-level description of the construction
workflow in this section. Annotators were trained
in task-specific rubrics and followed standardized
procedures to ensure validity and internal consistency. Each task is reviewed and validated by senior
educators. Detailed prompt formats and example
inputs are listed in Appendix C.4, and task-specific
annotation guidelines are available in Appendix E.

C.3.3

Application

Application-level tasks evaluate a model’s ability
to transfer learned knowledge to practical, contextrich educational settings. These tasks simulate authentic classroom scenarios where students are expected to solve problems, interpret structured information, make pedagogical classifications, or assess
open-ended work. The task set spans multimodal
dialogue interpretation, quantitative reasoning, and
evaluative writing feedback across primary to high
school levels.
Classroom classification tasks require models to
categorize utterances into instructional roles and
are evaluated using standard classification accuracy. Essay scoring tasks demand nuanced judgments along multiple dimensions such as coherence, grammar, and content relevance; models are
expected to assign scalar scores aligned with expertannotated rubrics. Mathematical problem-solving
tasks, ranging from basic arithmetic to multi-step
scientific applications, assess models’ ability to
process numerical and symbolic input in realistic educational formats. Depending on the task
structure, responses are evaluated via accuracy for
closed-form answers or correlation-based metrics
(e.g., QWK, Pearson) for open-ended assessment.
Classroom Dialogue Classification (3-1) This
task involves analyzing authentic classroom dialogue transcripts and classifying utterances into
pedagogically meaningful categories (e.g., teacher
explanation, student elaboration, questioning, or
feedback). The dialogues are sourced from real
middle school classrooms in subjects such as Information Technology, Mathematics, and Chinese.
Original classroom recordings were transcribed to
text and annotated by domain experts. The detailed
taxonomy used for classification is provided in Appendix E.
Primary Problem Solving (3-2) The primarylevel application tasks focus on basic quantitative
reasoning grounded in everyday life. Problem types
include simple arithmetic word problems, timedistance calculations, shopping-related questions,
and basic engineering/logistics scenarios adapted to
the cognitive level of elementary students. Content
is curated from elementary school test banks and
real classroom assessments.

Junior Problem Solving (3-3) Tasks at the junior high level include typical application-oriented
problems in Mathematics, Physics, and Chemistry.
These questions involve numerical calculation, data
interpretation, and conceptual application in scenarios such as physical motion, chemical change,
and geometric modeling. All questions are sourced
from historical Zhongkao exams, regional mock
tests, and online education platforms, reflecting the
procedural rigor of national curriculum standards.
Senior Problem Solving (3-4) At the high school
level, application tasks require multi-step reasoning, cross-topic integration, and realistic problem
contexts. Problems often span several knowledge
points across disciplines—for example, combining
algebra and geometry, or interpreting physics data
within real-life scientific experiments. Questions
are selected from Gaokao-level assessments and
authentic academic preparation resources, representing the most complex application scenarios in
the dataset.
Essay Scoring (3-5) This task evaluates the
model’s ability to assign scores to student-written
essays in accordance with expert grading. Essays
are collected from real classroom writing assignments in primary and middle school settings, covering narrative, argumentative, and descriptive genres. Each essay is paired with a reference score
given by certified instructors following standard
rubrics. Model responses are evaluated using QWK
and Pearson correlation with human scores.

C.3.6 Ethics
Ethics-level tasks are designed to assess a model’s
ability to make morally informed judgments and reflect normative reasoning in educational scenarios.
The tasks simulate real-world ethical dilemmas or
value-based decision-making processes, covering
individual conduct, social behavior, and professional responsibility. Prompts are carefully constructed by experts in moral education and educational psychology, with responses evaluated based
on alignment with educational norms, fairness, and
contextual sensitivity.
Ethics-level tasks are designed to assess a
model’s ability to make morally informed judgments and reflect normative reasoning in educational scenarios. These tasks simulate real-world
ethical dilemmas involving individual behavior,
community responsibility, and professional values.
Each task is carefully constructed by educational
or ethics experts and evaluated based on alignment
with commonly accepted moral standards in school
or societal contexts.
Primary Moral (6-1) This task focuses on
elementary-level moral understanding. Scenarios
involve basic ethical choices such as honesty, cooperation, empathy, and rule-following. The goal is to
evaluate whether a model can correctly distinguish
right from wrong in age-appropriate situations, including classroom behavior or helping others in
community settings. All questions are multiplechoice and presented in simplified language suitable for primary students.
Junior Ethics Scenario (6-2) At the junior high
level, ethical dilemmas are framed around interpersonal and family issues, such as fairness, peer conflict, and communication. Questions are designed
to reflect the psychosocial development stage of
adolescents and assess their reasoning about relationships, social roles, and responsibility in group
contexts.

Senior Ethics Scenario (6-3) Senior high school
tasks expand into themes of social responsibility,
civic engagement, and ethical leadership. Scenarios require reasoning about digital behavior, public
policy, or societal norms—for example, evaluating the ethics of reposting sensitive content online,
or considering actions to mitigate climate change.
These tasks demand higher-order moral judgment
and trade-off evaluation.
Educational Ethics Judgment (6-4) This task
simulates ethical decision-making in educational
professional settings, such as classroom equity,
grading fairness, and student-teacher dynamics.
It is intended for evaluating adult or pre-service
teacher alignment with ethical codes in educational
practice. Questions include dilemmas involving
bias, academic honesty, and responsibility toward
students’ well-being.

Guidelines for Expert Annotation

The expert annotation protocol was designed to systematically operationalize the EduAbility cognitive
scaffold through rigorous human judgment, ensuring that each benchmark item is not only pedagogically sound but also theoretically grounded. To this
end, we recruited 12 educational experts with an
average of 8.5 years of teaching experience across
primary, secondary, and higher education. All annotators possessed formal pedagogical training and
curriculum development experience within the Chinese educational system, making them uniquely
qualified to interpret and apply our nuanced cognitive framework.
The core of the annotation process was a multi-

stage validation pipeline, beginning with intensive
training where experts were familiarized with the
theoretical underpinnings of the EduAbility Taxonomy. The primary directive for all tasks was to
identify and label the dominant cognitive operation required by an item, thereby mapping it to a
specific level of the cognitive scaffold.
Task-specific guidelines were established to ensure this principle was applied consistently: For
Classroom Dialogue Classification, annotators
were instructed to move beyond surface-level semantics and analyze the full conversational flow—
including discourse function, speaker intention,
and pedagogical role. This holistic analysis was essential for classifying utterances according to their
function within the Application level of our framework (e.g., Guidance, Inference vs. simple Knowledge Recall). Ambiguous utterances were flagged
for consensus discussions to maintain theoretical
alignment. For Essay Scoring tasks, experts rated
compositions using official grading rubrics that
serve as practical proxies for the Application and
Creativity dimensions of the taxonomy. Dimensions such as content, coherence, and expressiveness were evaluated to produce a holistic score. To
ensure inter-rater reliability, annotators calibrated
their scoring in periodic review meetings, and only
essays with consensus scores or a variance within
±2 points were retained. For Ethical Judgment
tasks, the annotation process directly reflected the
design of the Ethics dimension in our taxonomy,
which prioritizes nuanced moral reasoning over
simple right/wrong dichotomies. Consequently,
these tasks were not labeled with "correct" answers.
Instead, experts constructed the full scenario and
option set to be pedagogically plausible and reflect
genuine moral ambiguity, drawing from real-world
dilemmas in teaching and school policy.
To ensure the integrity of this process, participants were compensated at a rate of ¥150 (about
$21) per hour and completed a paid training session
before formal annotation began. Across all tasks,
consistency was maintained through standardized
glossaries and shared exemplars. All annotations
underwent multi-round review by senior experts,
with correction logs kept to trace decision rationale.
This rigorous, human-led process was the crucial
preparatory step for the final AI-driven validation
performed by the Cognitive Alignment Verification Agent (as described in Section 3.2), ensuring
that the final dataset is a faithful operationalization
of the EduAbility framework.
