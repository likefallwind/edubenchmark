A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice, encouragement, or a proposal to "work through it together."

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, a specific method to try, a correct calculation with reasoning, or a specific conceptual redirect tied to the problem. Do not downgrade to "To some extent" simply because the tutor uses gentle phrasing like "let's take a closer look at..." or asks a clarifying question, or because the tutor ends with "let's try again" or "let's solve it together"—as long as they also provide a concrete focus, correct calculation, specific method, or conceptual redirect. Examples:
- Pointing to a specific step, fact, or part of the problem to redirect the student's attention, even if phrased as a gentle suggestion (e.g., "let's take a closer look at what happens to the other half of the 150 seeds that don't sprout - those might be our survivors", "let's take a closer look at your calculation, especially with the subtraction of 20 at the end", or "let's check if we can correctly identify the relationship between the second floor's storage space and the total storage space")
- Reminding the student of the specific objective or concept they should focus on, even without giving the answer (e.g., "remember we're looking for the greatest number between 5 and 3. Let's try again.")
- Explaining the specific procedural error and providing the correct calculation or equation (e.g., "If Sid had $4 more than half of his original money left, he would have $24 + $4 = $28 left, not $40. Let's try to correct the equation and solve it again." or "For the last wall, only four complete courses were built, not six. So, we should have 4 courses x 10 bricks/course = 40 bricks for the last wall. Let's adjust the calculations and try again.")
- Challenging an incorrect assumption and pointing out why it is unsupported (e.g., "No it does not. Why did you assume they need to win 80% of the games? The question mentions nothing as such.")
- Suggesting a specific method or approach for the student to try (e.g., "let's try adding 36 and 42 again, maybe we can use a hundreds chart or count up to find the correct answer")
- Working through the complete solution with reasoning, even if it confirms the student's work or is phrased collaboratively like "let's break it down together" (e.g., "to find the total number of games the Giants need to win, you correctly found that it's 2/3 of 30, which is 20 games, and since they already won 12, they indeed need to win 20 - 12 = 8 more games.")
- Asking a clarifying question that embeds the correct calculation or a specific conceptual redirect (e.g., "wouldn't that be 75 seeds that survive long enough to potentially flower?")
- A worked example that demonstrates the correct method with reasoning
- A specific procedural instruction tailored to the problem

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction or a specific focus, but the guidance is incomplete, vague, only partially tailored, or lacks the actual correction/information needed to move forward. Examples:
- Just stating the correct answer or fact without explaining the reasoning, calculation, or method (e.g., "when we compare 5 and 3, the greatest number among them is actually 5")
- Identifying the type of error without showing the correct method or calculation (e.g., "It seems you might have added the numbers instead of multiplying; let's try multiplying them again to find the volume.")
- Correcting a terminology mix-up that addresses a naming error but doesn't directly help solve the problem (e.g., "a five-sided polygon is called a pentagon, not an octagon which has eight sides. Let's review together!" or "Remember, an octagon has 8 sides; a 5-sided polygon is called a pentagon.")
- Giving a calculation or answer without identifying the student's specific error or explaining the reasoning behind it (e.g., "To find the total number of sacks after the second harvest, add the initial amount to the additional sacks: 24 sacks + 4.8 sacks = 28.8 sacks.")
- Pointing out that an error exists and mentioning the general topic, but without explaining what it is or how to fix it
- Reminding the student of a rule or constraint, but not explicitly correcting their procedure or telling them how to apply it to get the answer
- Stating a fact or definition without connecting it to the student's confusion or how to apply it
- Pointing out a misunderstanding and re-asking the original question without giving new information or a specific conceptual redirect (e.g., "it seems like you may have missed understanding the question; let's try again: which number is greater - 5 or 3?")
- General guidance not tailored to the specific problem (e.g., a textbook rule like "divide numerator by denominator")
- Working through a calculation or giving the answer without explaining the underlying reasoning or addressing the student's specific confusion
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation
- Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not completing it
- Pointing out a specific area of misunderstanding but just proposing to work through it together without giving a specific hint or new fact

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance, no specific focus, or merely points out a mistake without any helpful direction. Examples:
- Merely stating an answer is incorrect or pointing out an error exists without giving any new information, hint, or specific focus (e.g., "Your answer is incorrect", or "I noticed a small misinterpretation that affected your calculations; let's review the given information together")
- Merely identifying what the student did wrong or restating the problem objective without providing a specific hint, fact, or redirect to a specific part of the problem (e.g., "let's take a closer look at how we calculate the total after both harvests, not just the second harvest alone")
- Quoting or restating a fact directly from the problem statement to point out a reading error without explaining how to apply it or giving further direction (e.g., "remember that half of the original amount of fruit fell out, not half of the remaining fruit")
- Merely asking the student to "look again" or "try again" without giving any new information or specific focus
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any mathematical/content guidance
- Just proposing to work through something together without pointing to a specific area of confusion or giving any specific hint, fact, method, or focus (e.g., "let's work through this step-by-step to find the quotient of 72 divided by 6")

Note: A correction or worked example counts as Yes when it is correct, includes appropriate reasoning or calculation, and clearly addresses the student's confusion with a specific focus. Simply stating the correct answer without any reasoning, calculation, or method is "To some extent," not "Yes." Correcting only terminology (e.g., pentagon vs. octagon) without helping the student solve the problem is also "To some extent." The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
