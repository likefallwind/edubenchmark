A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new, actionable information—a specific value, step, method, or line of reasoning the student can immediately use—not just generic advice, encouragement, a proposal to "work through it together," or a restatement of what the problem already says.

**Yes** - The tutor provides concrete, correct, and relevant guidance that gives the student new, actionable information tied to the problem. This means a specific corrected value, a specific step or operation to perform, a worked example with reasoning, a clarifying question that walks through the logic to a specific conclusion, or a clear method the student can immediately apply. The student must receive something concrete they can act on—not just be told they are wrong or that they should look again. Examples:
- A worked example or calculation that demonstrates the correct method with reasoning (e.g., "the cost of the tickets minus the difference is $6.75 - $4.50 = $2.25 per ticket, and since they bought 3 tickets, they spent three times that amount")
- A clarifying question that walks through the reasoning and arrives at a specific conclusion (e.g., "if half of the 150 seeds sprout and are eaten, that means the other half are still remaining, so wouldn't that be 75 seeds that survive?")
- A direct factual correction that gives the student a specific corrected value or fact they can immediately use (e.g., "Hillary buys the Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday editions for $0.50 each, not $1.50 per day")
- A specific procedural instruction tailored to the problem (e.g., "divide 72 by 6 to find the correct number of doughnuts")
- A specific hint that gives the student a concrete fact, step, or focus tied to the problem (e.g., "the 75 sprouted seeds are already included in the 150")
- Identifying a specific calculation error and suggesting a specific alternative method (e.g., "let's try adding 36 and 42 again, maybe we can use a hundreds chart or count up")
- Setting up an approach with clear reasoning and a concrete direction, even if the explanation is cut off mid-sentence (e.g., walking through the logic for setting up an equation)

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction, but the guidance is incomplete, vague, only partially tailored, or doesn't give the student enough concrete information to make clear progress. Examples:
- Pointing out a constraint or error area without explaining what specifically is wrong or how to fix it (e.g., "remember the number of candles should be a whole number, so let's recheck the division")
- Identifying the general area of confusion and proposing to look at it together without giving a specific hint, value, or method (e.g., "I think there's a misunderstanding about calculating the total distance—let's take another look at the problem together")
- Correcting terminology or naming without helping the student make progress on the underlying problem itself (e.g., "a 5-sided polygon is a pentagon, not an octagon")
- General guidance not tailored to the specific problem (e.g., a textbook rule like "divide numerator by denominator")
- Suggesting an approach without actually working through it or tying it to the student's specific confusion
- Stating a fact or definition without connecting it to how to apply it
- Working through a calculation or giving the answer without explaining the underlying reasoning or addressing the student's specific confusion
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation (the tutor is still engaged with the problem, but the guidance itself is flawed)
- Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not giving concrete direction

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance or new actionable information. Examples:
- Merely telling the student their answer is incorrect without any hint, explanation, or direction (e.g., "Sorry, your answer is incorrect")
- Merely asking the student to "look again," "try again," or "what's the next step?" without giving any new information, hint, or explanation
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any specific guidance
- Just proposing to work through something together without giving a specific instruction, hint, fact, or method (e.g., "let's work through this step-by-step to find the quotient of 72 divided by 6"—contrast with "divide 72 by 6 to find the correct number of doughnuts," which IS a specific instruction and counts as Yes)
- A bare correction that merely negates the student's approach or restates a problem constraint without giving a new, concrete value, step, or method (e.g., "half of the original amount of fruit fell out, not half of the remaining"—this restates the problem's wording without telling the student what value to use, what step to take, or how to apply the correction)

Note: The key distinction for Yes is whether the tutor gives the student concrete, new, actionable information—a specific value, step, method, or worked reasoning they can immediately use. A correction that only negates the student's approach or restates what the problem already says does not count, even if it correctly identifies the error. Conversely, a direct instruction or corrected value counts as Yes even when phrased conversationally (e.g., "let's try again—divide 72 by 6"). The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
