A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice or encouragement.

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, direction, or a useful focus tied to the problem. Do not downgrade to "To some extent" simply because the tutor uses gentle phrasing like "let's..." or "remember..." as long as they provide a concrete focus, correct information, or a specific hint. Examples:
- Directly stating the correct approach, calculation, or constraint that addresses the student's error (e.g., "remember we're looking for the greatest number between 5 and 3", "the 20% increase for the second harvest should be calculated from the original yield of 20 sacks, not from the increased yield of 24 sacks", "remember the question asks for the average speed needed for the remaining portion of the drive, not the overall trip", "remember that half of the original amount of fruit fell out, not half of the remaining fruit")
- Explaining a specific procedural error and how to correct it, providing the correct calculation or equation with reasoning (e.g., "if each ticket cost $6.75, then the amount spent on food would actually be 6.75 - 4.50 = 2.25... it would be more accurate to represent this as 20.25 - 4.50, since 20.25 is the total amount spent on tickets", or "For the last wall, only four complete courses were built, not six. So, we should have 4 courses x 10 bricks/course = 40 bricks for the last wall")
- Suggesting a specific method or approach for fixing a calculation error (e.g., "let's try adding 36 and 42 again, maybe we can use a hundreds chart or count up to find the correct answer")
- Pointing to a specific step, fact, or part of the problem and giving a hint or new information (e.g., "let's take a closer look at what happens to the other half of the 150 seeds that don't sprout - those might be our survivors", "let's take a closer look at your calculation, especially with the subtraction of 20 at the end")
- A worked example that demonstrates the correct method with reasoning
- A direct factual correction that addresses the student's specific error or misconception
- A specific procedural instruction tailored to the problem

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction or identifies a specific area of confusion, but the guidance is incomplete, lacks the actual correction, or doesn't give enough specific information to fully move forward. Examples:
- Identifying a specific area of misunderstanding and proposing to look at it together, but without directly stating the correct approach or giving a specific hint (e.g., "let's take a closer look at how the 20% increase applies to the original 20 sacks from the second harvest, rather than the total from the first harvest", "let's take a closer look at how we calculate the total after both harvests, not just the second harvest alone")
- Giving minimal direction without specific guidance (e.g., "Please recheck your work")
- Asking the student to explain their reasoning without providing new information or a specific hint (e.g., "Could you explain how you got 4?")
- Correcting terminology or a surface detail that doesn't directly help solve the problem (e.g., "a five-sided polygon is actually called a pentagon. An octagon is an eight-sided polygon.")
- Reminding the student of what the question asks for in general terms without identifying the specific calculation error or how to fix it (e.g., "the question asks for the total number of tickets Connie redeemed, not just the number spent on the koala")
- Pointing out that an error exists and mentioning the general topic, but without explaining what it is or how to fix it
- Reminding the student of a rule or constraint, but not explicitly correcting their procedure or telling them how to apply it to get the answer
- Stating a fact or definition without connecting it to the student's confusion or how to apply it
- General guidance not tailored to the specific problem (e.g., a textbook rule like "divide numerator by denominator")
- Working through a calculation or giving the answer without explaining the underlying reasoning or addressing the student's specific confusion
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation
- Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not completing it

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance, no specific focus, or no engagement with the problem content. Examples:
- Merely stating an answer is incorrect without giving any new information, hint, or direction (e.g., "Your answer is incorrect")
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any specific guidance or direction
- Just proposing to work through something together without pointing to any specific area of confusion, hint, fact, method, or focus (e.g., "let's work through this step-by-step to find the quotient of 72 divided by 6")
- Responses that contain no engagement with the problem content at all

Note: A direct correction, worked example, or specific reminder of a problem constraint counts as Yes when it is correct and clearly addresses the student's confusion with appropriate reasoning or focus. The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
