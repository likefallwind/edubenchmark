A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice, encouragement, or a proposal to "work through it together."

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, direction, or a useful, actionable focus tied to the problem. Examples:
- A clarifying question that directs the student to show or explain specific work, or to check, identify, or verify a specific relationship, step, or element in the problem (e.g., "Can you show me how you arrived at 6?" or "Let's check if we can correctly identify the relationship between the second floor's storage space and the total storage space first")
- Identifying a specific error and showing the correct calculation or approach with reasoning (e.g., "If Sid had $4 more than half of his original money left, he would have $24 + $4 = $28 left, not $40. Let's correct the equation and solve it again")
- A direct factual correction that fully resolves the student's specific error or misconception (e.g., "Actually, the greatest of 5 and 3 is 5")
- A specific hint that points to the relevant concept, step, fact, or part of the problem and gives the student a clear direction (e.g., "the 75 sprouted seeds are already included in the 150")
- A reminder of a specific rule or constraint relevant to the problem (e.g., "the break doesn't count towards driving time—calculate each session separately, then add")
- A worked example that demonstrates the correct method with reasoning
- A specific procedural instruction tailored to the problem (e.g., "line up the numbers correctly, add the ones place, carry over if needed")

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction or new information, but the guidance is incomplete, vague, only partially tailored, or flawed. Examples:
- Giving the answer or stating the method without explaining the underlying reasoning (e.g., "find the GCD of 48 and 60, which is 12; the largest group is 12")
- Identifying an error and suggesting the correct approach but not working through it or fully explaining it (e.g., "you added instead of multiplied; let's try multiplying them again to find the volume")
- Stating a correction as a fact without showing the calculation or explaining how to apply it to solve the problem (e.g., "remember to count Carmen's initial bid of $200 as one of her bids too, so she actually bid four times")
- Pointing to a relevant element of the problem with a hint but framing it as "let's think about X" without giving a concrete action or specific direction (e.g., "let's think about how many bids were made in total, including Carmen's winning bid")
- Suggesting an approach or strategy without actually working through it or tying it to the student's specific confusion
- Stating a fact or definition without connecting it to how to apply it
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation
- Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not completing it

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance or the guidance is too vague to help the student move forward. Examples:
- Merely saying the answer is "incorrect" or "wrong" without any explanation, hint, or direction (e.g., "Sorry, your answer is incorrect")
- "Let's reconsider X" or "look again" that merely restates what to calculate or check, without giving any new information, hint, or specific direction (e.g., "let's reconsider the total number of games they'll have played by the end of the season and what 2/3 of that total would be")
- Pointing out what was overlooked or where an error might be, without explaining how to fix it, why it matters, or what to do next (e.g., "you might have overlooked including the graduates themselves in the total count of attendees")
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any mathematical/content guidance
- Just proposing to work through something together without giving any specific hint, fact, method, or focus (e.g., "let's work through this step-by-step to find the quotient of 72 divided by 6")
- Repeating a step the student already performed, followed by generic advice like "verify it makes sense," without identifying the error or providing new direction
- Guidance that trails off or is cut off mid-explanation without completing the thought or providing usable direction
- Correcting only a surface detail such as terminology or naming that does not help the student make progress on the problem itself

Note: The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer. A direct correction or worked example counts as Yes when it identifies the specific error and shows the correct approach or calculation with reasoning. Merely stating a correction without showing how to apply it is To some extent.
