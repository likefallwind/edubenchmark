A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice, encouragement, or a proposal to "work through it together."

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, direction, or a useful focus tied to the problem. Examples:
- A specific hint or clarification pointing to the relevant concept, step, fact, or part of the problem
- Explaining the specific procedural error and how to correct it (e.g., "you actually need to multiply the number of dozens by the cost per dozen, then add those amounts together, not add the individual costs before multiplying by the number of items in a dozen")
- Pointing to a specific part of the problem to redirect the student's attention (e.g., "let's take a closer look at what happens to the other half of the 150 seeds that don't sprout")
- Challenging an unfounded assumption with a targeted question (e.g., "Why did you assume they need to win 80% of the games? The question mentions nothing as such.")
- A worked example that demonstrates the correct method with reasoning (e.g., walking through the calculation of 6.75 - 4.50 = 2.25 and explaining why it's better to use 20.25)
- A direct factual correction that addresses the student's specific mathematical error or misconception (e.g., "you only need to subtract the bricks for the two courses that weren't built on the last wall, not for all four walls")
- A specific procedural instruction tailored to the problem (e.g., "line up the numbers correctly, add the ones place, carry over if needed")

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction, but the guidance is incomplete, vague, only partially tailored, or just points out a fact without applying it. Examples:
- Pointing out that an error exists without explaining what it is or how to fix it
- Asking the student to explain their reasoning or prompting them without giving new information (e.g., "Could you explain how you got 4?")
- Pointing out a constraint or reminding the student of a rule, but not explicitly correcting their procedure or telling them how to apply it to get the answer (e.g., "remember that Carmen only bids after the other three people, so she doesn't make every bid")
- Giving a brief hint that points to a missing step without explanation (e.g., "Remember to include the Sunday paper too")
- Stating a fact or definition without connecting it to the student's confusion or how to apply it (e.g., "a five-sided polygon is actually called a pentagon. An octagon is an eight-sided polygon")
- General guidance not tailored to the specific problem (e.g., a textbook rule like "divide numerator by denominator")
- Working through a calculation or giving the answer without explaining the underlying reasoning or addressing the student's specific confusion
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation
- Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not completing it
- Giving a partial hint and asking the student to "try again from there" without further explanation

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance. Examples:
- Merely asking the student to "look again" or "try again" without giving any new information, hint, or explanation
- Correcting only a surface detail such as terminology or naming that does not help the student make progress on the problem itself (e.g., "the correct term for a 5-sided polygon is actually a pentagon")
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any mathematical/content guidance
- Just proposing to work through something together without giving any specific hint, fact, method, or focus (e.g., "let's work through this step-by-step to find the quotient of 72 divided by 6")

Note: A direct correction or worked example counts as Yes when it is correct and clearly addresses the student's confusion with appropriate reasoning or focus. The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
