A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, explanation, worked example, clarifying question, or direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice, encouragement, or a vague proposal to "work through it together."

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, direction, or a useful focus tied to the problem. Do not downgrade to "To some extent" simply because the tutor uses gentle phrasing like "let's take a closer look at..." or asks a clarifying question, as long as they provide a concrete focus or correct information. Examples:
- Pointing to a specific step, fact, calculation, or part of the problem to redirect the student's attention, even if phrased as a gentle suggestion or question. As long as the tutor identifies the exact area of focus, it counts as Yes (e.g., "let's take a closer look at the last wall's calculation together to make sure we're interpreting the problem correctly", "let's take a step back and think about what we know for sure about the tickets Connie spent on items other than the koala bear", or "let's make sure we understand the logic behind the total number of bids correctly").
- Explaining the specific procedural error and how to correct it, providing the correct calculation or equation (e.g., "the 20% increase for the second harvest should be calculated from the original yield of 20 sacks, not from the increased yield of 24 sacks", or "when we round 17 to the nearest ten, we look at the ones digit, and since it's less than 5, we round down to 20, not up to 30").
- Reminding the student of a specific procedural rule tailored to fixing their error (e.g., "let's recall how to add multi-digit numbers, we need to line up the numbers correctly and then add the ones place, carrying over if needed").
- Correcting a specific incorrect assumption or reading error directly (e.g., "Why did you assume they need to win 80% of the games? The question mentions nothing as such.").
- Walking through the logic of the problem step-by-step and setting up an approach.
- A direct factual correction that addresses the student's specific mathematical error or misconception.

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction or identifies an error, but the guidance is incomplete, lacks a concrete focus, or points out the error without giving a specific hint or new fact on how to move forward. Examples:
- Pointing out a reading error or restating the problem objective without providing a specific hint, fact, or step to fix it (e.g., "Remember, the question asks for the *total* number of tickets Connie redeemed, not just the number spent on the koala.").
- Proposing to look at an alternative approach without explaining it or giving a concrete hint on how to apply it (e.g., "let's take a closer look at how we can use the ratio to directly calculate the number of blue candles without dividing by the total parts.").
- Pointing out that an error exists and mentioning the general topic, but without explaining what it is or how to fix it.
- Correcting a terminology mix-up that addresses a conceptual confusion but doesn't directly solve the problem.
- General guidance not tailored to the specific problem (e.g., a textbook rule like "divide numerator by denominator").
- Working through a calculation or giving the answer without explaining the underlying reasoning or addressing the student's specific confusion.
- Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation.

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance, no specific focus, or merely points out a mistake without any helpful direction. Examples:
- Merely stating an answer is incorrect or pointing out an error exists without giving any new information, hint, or specific focus. Proposing to "review" or "work through" something together without pointing to a specific area of confusion (e.g., "Your answer is incorrect", or "I noticed a small misinterpretation that affected your calculations; let's review the given information together").
- Merely asking the student to "look again" or "try again" without giving any new information or pointing to a specific step.
- Correcting only a surface detail such as terminology that does not help the student make progress on the problem itself.
- Generic encouragement or restating the problem without any mathematical/content guidance.

Note: A direct correction or worked example counts as Yes when it is correct and clearly addresses the student's confusion with appropriate reasoning or focus. The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
