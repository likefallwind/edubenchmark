A tutor reply "provides guidance" when it gives the student concrete, correct, and relevant help making progress on the problem—via a hint, an explanation, a worked example, a targeted clarifying question, or a direct correction. The guidance must be specific to the problem and must give the student new information, a clear direction, or a useful focus that helps them move forward—not just generic advice, encouragement, or an empty proposal to "work through it together."

**Yes** - The tutor provides specific, correct, and relevant guidance that directly helps the student move forward. The guidance gives new information, direction, or a useful focus tied to the problem. Examples:
- **Targeted clarifying questions**: Asking a question that points to a specific flaw, error, or contradiction in the student's work (e.g., "can you show me how you arrived at 6?", or "if Jason drove at 60 mph for the entire trip, would he arrive in 1 hour 30 minutes as the question states?").
- **Specific procedural corrections**: Telling the student exactly what to do differently to fix their error (e.g., "you only need to subtract the bricks for the two courses that weren't built on the *last* wall, not for all four walls", or "instead of adding the 4 and 9 hours, we need to multiply the 60 miles per hour rate by the total number of hours").
- **Diagnosing the conceptual error**: Explicitly identifying what the student did wrong conceptually (e.g., "You gave a number in the middle of 3 and 5, not the biggest").
- **Worked examples / explanations**: Walking through the correct method, steps, or reasoning (e.g., "to find the total games, you correctly found 2/3 of 30, which is 20, and since they won 12, they need 8 more").
*Note: Even if the tutor says "let's do X together," if they actually provide the method, explanation, or correction, it counts as Yes.*

**To some extent** - The tutor engages with the problem and provides some direction, but the guidance is incomplete, vague, only partially tailored, or flawed. Examples:
- **Vague direction**: Pointing out a constraint or fact and suggesting an action without giving the method or explaining how to apply it (e.g., "remember the number of candles must be a whole number, so let's recheck the division of red candles into sets").
- **Surface corrections**: Correcting terminology or naming conventions that do not help solve the mathematical problem itself (e.g., "a five-sided polygon is actually called a pentagon").
- **Incomplete setups**: Recapping known information and beginning to set up an approach but not completing it.
- **Flawed guidance**: Guidance that contains an error in reasoning or calculation (the tutor is still engaged with the problem, but the guidance itself is flawed).

**No** - The tutor provides no actual guidance that helps the student move forward. Examples:
- **Blank rejections**: Just stating the answer is incorrect (e.g., "Your answer is incorrect").
- **Bare facts**: Stating a rule or fact without explaining how to apply it, diagnosing the student's specific error, or telling them what to do next (e.g., "remember that half of the **original** amount of fruit fell out, not half of the **remaining** fruit").
- **Empty proposals**: Proposing to look at something or work through something together without giving any new information, concrete method, or specific actionable step (e.g., "let's take a closer look at how we calculate the total after both harvests, not just the second harvest alone").
- **Generic encouragement**: "Try again" or "look again" without any specific hint.

Note: A direct correction or worked example counts as Yes when it is correct and clearly addresses the student's confusion with appropriate reasoning or focus. The grading reflects whether concrete, correct, specific guidance is provided—not whether the tutor avoids giving the answer.
