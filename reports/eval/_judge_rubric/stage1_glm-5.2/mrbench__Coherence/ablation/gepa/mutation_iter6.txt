- **Yes**: The reply is logically coherent, internally self-consistent, and directly addresses the current topic or problem, following naturally from the conversation. The following are all fully coherent and should not be downgraded: correcting a student's specific error (including when quoting or referencing the student's own wrong answer); providing heuristics or reasoning strategies (e.g., "when we add two numbers, the result is usually larger"); asking guiding or scaffolding questions; acknowledging and incorporating a student's correction; offering brief hints or nudges; brief affirmations, encouragements, or acknowledgments (e.g., "That was a very good try."); brief, direct directives to guide the student (e.g., "Kindly recheck your answer."); or moving to a new topic after the current problem has been resolved, provided the transition is clear and specific (e.g., "Now let's move on to understanding how to solve quadratic equations using the factoring method"). Do not penalize for simple mathematical calculation errors, pedagogical simplifications, basic questions, typos, mentioning the student's own wrong answers, or slightly confusing phrasing if the underlying logic is sound and on-topic.

- **To some extent**: The reply is on-topic and broadly consistent but has minor coherence flaws. This includes: being overly generic or fragmented without engaging the specific problem (e.g., "Ok, remember there are only 12 eggs to begin with"); stating a fact, procedure, or calculation step without connecting it to the conversation or fully working through the logic (e.g., "we need to divide the total seconds in a minute (60) by the time it takes to sharpen one pencil"); affirming a conclusion or introducing a next step without fully grounding it in the context (e.g., "let's calculate Kristin's reading time based on Peter's" or "Let's think about how to calculate Kristin's reading time"); making slight logical leaps or flawed reasoning that disrupts the internal logic (e.g., "Since Peter reads three times as fast as Kristin, it would actually take Kristin less time to read a book, not more"); introducing gamification that disrupts logical progression; or introducing unverified past events (hallucinations) that distract from the current problem.

- **No**: The reply is fundamentally incoherent, contradictory, or off-topic. This includes: contradicting itself (e.g., praising a student's approach while simultaneously redirecting away from that very approach); giving guidance that conflicts with the problem's requirements; abruptly abandoning the current problem without resolution or a clear, specific transition (e.g., "Great job! Let's move on to the next problem." with no specifics); or otherwise breaking the logical continuity of the conversation.
