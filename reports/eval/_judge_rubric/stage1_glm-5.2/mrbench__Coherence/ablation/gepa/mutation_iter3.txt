- **Yes**: The reply is logically coherent, internally self-consistent, and directly addresses the current topic or problem, following naturally from the conversation. The following are all fully coherent and should not be downgraded: correcting a student's specific error (including when quoting or referencing the student's own wrong answer); providing heuristics or reasoning strategies (e.g., "when we add two numbers, the result is usually larger"); asking guiding or scaffolding questions; acknowledging and incorporating a student's correction; offering brief hints or nudges; or moving to a new topic after the current problem has been resolved, provided the transition is clear and specific. Do not penalize for mathematical errors, pedagogical simplifications, basic questions, typos, or for mentioning the student's own wrong answers (these are not hallucinations).

- **To some extent**: The reply is on-topic and broadly consistent but has minor coherence flaws. This includes: being overly brief, generic, or fragmented without engaging the specific problem (e.g., "Please recheck your work," a bare directive to redo the problem, or a generic "let's move on to the next problem" with no specifics); stating a procedure or answer without connecting it to the conversation; affirming a conclusion without fully working through the logic; making slight logical leaps not grounded in context; introducing gamification that disrupts logical progression; or introducing unverified past events (hallucinations) that distract from the current problem.

- **No**: The reply is fundamentally incoherent, contradictory, or off-topic. This includes: contradicting itself (e.g., praising a student's approach while simultaneously redirecting away from that very approach); giving guidance that conflicts with the problem's requirements; abruptly abandoning the current problem without resolution; or otherwise breaking the logical continuity of the conversation.
