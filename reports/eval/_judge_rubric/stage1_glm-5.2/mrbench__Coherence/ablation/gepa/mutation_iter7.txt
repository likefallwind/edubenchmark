- **Yes**: The reply is logically coherent, internally self-consistent, and directly addresses the current topic or problem, following naturally from the conversation. The following are fully coherent and should not be downgraded: correcting a student's specific error (including when quoting or referencing the student's own wrong answer); providing heuristics or reasoning strategies (e.g., "when we add two numbers, the result is usually larger"); asking guiding or scaffolding questions; acknowledging and incorporating a student's correction (e.g., "I appreciate you catching your mistake"); offering brief hints or nudges; providing brief, generic encouragement (e.g., "That was a very good try") or brief directives (e.g., "Kindly recheck your answer"); or moving to a new topic after the current problem has been resolved, provided the transition is clear and specific. Do not penalize for pure calculation errors (e.g., arithmetic mistakes), pedagogical simplifications, typos, or for mentioning the student's own wrong answers (these are not hallucinations).

- **To some extent**: The reply is on-topic and broadly consistent but has minor coherence flaws. This includes: being overly brief or fragmented without engaging the specific problem (though simple encouragement or brief directives like "recheck your answer" are fully coherent); stating a correct procedure or formula without connecting it to the specific values or context of the problem; affirming a conclusion without fully working through the logic; making slight logical leaps not grounded in context; introducing gamification that disrupts logical progression; introducing unverified past events (hallucinations) that distract from the current problem; or providing reasoning that contains a logical contradiction in its deduction (e.g., incorrectly deducing that a faster reader takes less time).

- **No**: The reply is fundamentally incoherent, contradictory, or off-topic. This includes: contradicting itself (e.g., praising a student's approach while simultaneously redirecting away from that very approach); abruptly abandoning the current problem without resolution (e.g., a generic "Great job! Let's move on to the next problem" without specifically resolving the current one); giving guidance that directly conflicts with the problem's logical or algebraic requirements (e.g., telling a student to divide when the problem requires working backwards and multiplying); or otherwise breaking the logical continuity of the conversation.
