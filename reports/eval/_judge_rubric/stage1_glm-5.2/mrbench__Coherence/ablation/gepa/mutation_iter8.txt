- **Yes**: The reply is logically coherent, internally self-consistent, and directly addresses the current topic or problem, following naturally from the conversation. The following are all fully coherent and should not be downgraded: briefly acknowledging a student's attempt (e.g., "That was a good try."); providing brief hints or nudges, including generic directives to recheck work (e.g., "Kindly recheck your answer"); correcting a student's specific error (including when quoting or referencing the student's own wrong answer); providing heuristics or reasoning strategies (e.g., "when we add two numbers, the result is usually larger"); asking guiding or scaffolding questions (e.g., "Let's double-check how many toys Samantha gets at half price"); acknowledging and incorporating a student's correction; or moving to a new topic after the current problem has been resolved, provided the transition is clear and specific (e.g., "Now let's move on to understanding how to solve quadratic equations using the factoring method"). Do not penalize for mathematical errors, pedagogical simplifications, slightly confusing phrasing, or for mentioning the student's own wrong answers (these are not hallucinations), provided the underlying intent and logical progression remain clear.

- **To some extent**: The reply is broadly on-topic but has minor coherence flaws. This includes: being fragmented, grammatically incoherent, or hard to parse without abandoning the topic; making slight logical leaps or stating a procedure without fully grounding it in the specific problem context; containing minor logical contradictions or confusions that do not completely derail the conversation (e.g., misstating an intermediate relationship but still guiding the student to recalculate); introducing gamification that disrupts logical progression; or introducing unverified past events (hallucinations) that distract from the current problem.

- **No**: The reply is fundamentally incoherent, contradictory, or off-topic. This includes: stating a disconnected, generic procedure or formula with no specific connection to the problem (e.g., "To find the half-price toy, divide the original price by 2"); abruptly abandoning the current problem without resolution or clear transition (e.g., a bare "Let's move on to the next problem" with no specifics); abruptly shifting to an unrelated or non-sequitur question (e.g., "Now, can you tell me what is the sum of 18 degrees and 30 degrees?"); contradicting itself; giving guidance that conflicts with the problem's requirements; or otherwise breaking the logical continuity of the conversation.
