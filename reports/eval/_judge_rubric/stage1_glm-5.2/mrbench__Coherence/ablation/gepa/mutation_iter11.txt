- **Yes**: The reply is logically coherent, internally self-consistent, and directly addresses the current topic or problem, following naturally from the conversation. The following are all fully coherent and should not be downgraded: briefly acknowledging a student's attempt (e.g., "That was a good try."); correcting a student's specific error (including when quoting or referencing the student's own wrong answer) and explaining the direct calculation or logic (e.g., "there are actually 6 students in each row, not just 4, so we need to recalculate"; "the remaining eggs also have one yolk each!"); providing heuristics or reasoning strategies; asking guiding or scaffolding questions; acknowledging and incorporating a student's correction; or moving to a new problem after the current one is resolved with a clear, collaborative transition (e.g., "Great job! Now let's move on to the next problem together."). Do not penalize for mathematical errors, pedagogical simplifications, run-on sentences, slightly confusing phrasing, grammatical flaws, missing punctuation, typos (e.g., "multiplcation"), or factual mistakes (e.g., misstating a place value), provided the underlying intent and logical progression remain clear.

- **To some extent**: The reply is broadly on-topic but has minor coherence flaws. This includes: giving bare, unspecified directives or reminders that lack specific grounding in the problem context (e.g., "Please recheck your work."; "Ok, remember there are only 12 eggs to begin with"); making slight logical leaps or providing vague guidance to recalculate without fully grounding it in the specific problem context (e.g., "let's take another look at how we calculate Kristin's reading time based on Peter's"); stating a bare procedure without fully grounding it in the specific numbers of the problem (e.g., "we need to divide the total seconds in a minute (60) by the time it takes to sharpen one pencil"); containing highly repetitive, redundant, or circular explanations that make the logical progression slightly hard to follow (e.g., repeating the same calculation rationale multiple times in one sentence, such as "divide 60 by 45 to find out how many 45-second intervals... and that is 60/45"); containing minor logical contradictions or confusions that do not completely derail the conversation; introducing gamification that distracts from the logical progression (e.g., "Plus 1 point for your efforts"); or introducing unverified past events (minor hallucinations) that distract from the current problem but do not change the core requirements.

- **No**: The reply is fundamentally incoherent, contradictory, or off-topic. This includes: stating a disconnected, generic procedure or formula with no specific connection to the problem; abruptly abandoning the current problem without resolution or a specific, collaborative transition (e.g., "Great job! Let's move on to the next problem."); abruptly shifting to an unrelated or non-sequitur question (e.g., "Now, can you tell me what is the sum of 18 degrees and 30 degrees?"); contradicting itself; hallucinating a core problem constraint that fundamentally conflicts with the problem's requirements (e.g., stating "they are bought 2 at a time" when this changes the problem's logic); giving guidance that conflicts with the problem's requirements or completely misinterprets the core premise of the problem (e.g., using "two-thirds full" instead of "two-thirds empty", leading to fundamentally flawed guidance); or otherwise breaking the logical continuity of the conversation.
