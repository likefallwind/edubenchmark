In baking, whole poppy seeds add flavor, texture, and visual contrast to breads, rolls, bagels, crackers, muffins, cakes, and cookies. They can be mixed into dough or batter, sprinkled over surfaces before baking, or combined with ingredients such as lemon, almonds, or honey. They are especially common in Central and Eastern European baked goods. Ground poppy seeds are used to make dense, flavorful fillings for pastries such as strudels, kolaches, hamantaschen, and Polish makowiec. The seeds are often cooked with milk and sweetened with sugar or honey. In South Asian cooking, particularly Indian cuisine, white poppy seeds may be ground into pastes to thicken curries, kormas, and sauces while adding richness and a subtle flavor. Poppy seeds also have savory uses beyond baking. They can be added to salad dressings, vegetable dishes, noodles, rice, and spice mixtures, or toasted briefly to enhance their aroma. Their crunch makes them useful as a topping, while ground seeds can improve the body and texture of sauces without requiring large amounts of flour or other starches. The seeds can be pressed to produce poppyseed oil. This oil has limited culinary use in some regions and is also used by artists as a binder in oil paints. It dries more slowly and generally yellows less than linseed oil, making it useful for particular painting applications. Seed oil may additionally appear in some cosmetic or soap formulations. Nutritionally, poppy seeds provide unsaturated fats, fiber, and minerals such as calcium and manganese, although typical culinary servings are small. Food-grade seeds should be obtained from reputable suppliers. Clean seeds contain little opiate material, but contamination can occur during harvesting; unwashed seeds and poppy-seed tea can be dangerous. Eating poppy-seed foods can also occasionally interfere with opioid drug tests, and the seeds should not be used as a homemade treatment for pain or insomnia. Summary: Poppy seeds are used mainly to flavor and decorate baked goods, make sweet pastry fillings, thicken savory dishes, and add crunch to foods. They also yield oil with culinary, artistic, and limited cosmetic applications. Their principal value is culinary rather than medicinal, and only food-grade seeds should be consumed.
