基准介绍

TutorBench (ScaleAI) evaluates LLM tutoring on real STEM tasks across three use cases -- adaptive explanation, assessment & feedback, and active-learning hint generation -- in text-only and multimodal form. Each sample carries expert-written rubric criteria weighted by severity (critical +5, not_critical +1, critical_negative -5).

The model under test produces a tutoring reply; a fixed LLM judge (TUTORBENCH_JUDGE_MODEL, default MiniMax-M3) rates every criterion pass/fail. The headline is the weighted average rubric rating ARR_w (paper Eq. 1); simple pass rate, positive-weighted rate, and a shifted variant are reported alongside.

This is a faithful re-implementation of the official protocol on the full public set, not a reproduction of the internal Fair815 leaderboard (Claude Sonnet 4 judge, private 815-sample subset) -- numbers are not comparable to those.

主页：https://huggingface.co/datasets/ScaleAI/TutorBench

#68096a17d9108896c270f6b6use_case=UC1 modality=text subject=Physics
Initial question: A space station is in an elliptical orbit above planet X, which has a mass of $M$. The space station has a mass of $m$, and a velocity of $v_0$ at the closest approach distance $r_1$. Determine the velocity of the space station at its maximum distance from planet X, $r_2$. Assume that the gravitational potential energy is zero when masses are infinitely far apart. Tutor's initial explanation: # Finding Velocity at Maximum Orbital Distance This problem can be solved using two conservation principles: energy conservation and angular momentum conservation. ## Step 1: Understand what we know - Mass of planet X: $M$ - Mass of space station: $m$ - Velocity at closest approach: $v_0$ - Closest distance (periapsis): $r_1$ - Maximum distance (apoapsis): $r_2$ - Zero potential energy reference at infinite separation ## Step 2: Apply conservation of angular momentum At both the periapsis and apoapsis, the velocity is perpendicular to the radius vector in an elliptical orbit. Angular momentum at periapsis: $L_1 = mr_1v_0$ Angular momentum at apoapsis: $L_2 = mr_2v_2$ Since angular momentum is conserved: $L_1 = L_2$ Therefore: $mr_1v_0 = mr_2v_2$ Solving for $v_2$: $v_2 = \frac{r_1v_0}{r_2}$ This is our answer! The velocity at the maximum distance is $v_2 = \frac{r_1v_0}{r_2}$. We could verify this using energy conservation, but the angular momentum approach gives us the direct solution. Student's follow-up: Hold on, I'm confused! I tried using conservation of energy and found a completely different value, $v_2=\sqrt{v_1^2-\frac{GM}{r_1}+\frac{GM}{r_2}}$. How do you reconcile those two results?

#68096a17da7edca154c70832use_case=UC1 modality=text subject=Chemistry
Initial question: Find the order of the decomposition reaction of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. The concentration of the reactant follows the following trend: * At time 0 seconds, the concentration is 0.4 M * At time 100 seconds, the concentration is 0.283 M * At time 200 seconds, the concentration is 0.200 M * At time 300 seconds, the concentration is 0.141 M Tutor's initial explanation: # Finding the Order of Hydrogen Peroxide Decomposition To determine the order of a reaction, I need to see which rate law the data follows. For the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ Let me analyze the concentration data to determine if this follows zero-order, first-order, or second-order kinetics. ## Step 1: Organize the data | Time (s) | [H₂O₂] (M) | |----------|------------| | 0 | 0.400 | | 100 | 0.283 | | 200 | 0.200 | | 300 | 0.141 | ## Step 2: Test for first-order kinetics For a first-order reaction, ln[A] vs. time should give a straight line. | Time (s) | [H₂O₂] (M) | ln[H₂O₂] | |----------|------------|-----------| | 0 | 0.400 | -0.916 | | 100 | 0.283 | -1.262 | | 200 | 0.200 | -1.609 | | 300 | 0.141 | -1.959 | Calculating the slope between consecutive points: - From 0 to 100s: -0.00346 per second - From 100 to 200s: -0.00347 per second - From 200 to 300s: -0.00350 per second These slopes are very consistent, strongly suggesting a first-order reaction. ## Step 3: Verify by testing second-order kinetics For a second-order reaction, 1/[A] vs. time should give a straight line. | Time (s) | 1/[H₂O₂] (M⁻¹) | |----------|----------------| | 0 | 2.50 | | 100 | 3.53 | | 200 | 5.00 | | 300 | 7.09 | The slopes between consecutive points increase significantly, indicating this is not second-order. ## Conclusion The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen follows first-order kinetics, where the rate = k[H₂O₂]. The rate constant k ≈ 0.00347 s⁻¹, calculated from the slope of the ln[H₂O₂] vs. time plot. Student's follow-up: Ok, but we only have like 5 data points at constant intervals, so how can we be absolutely sure it's not zero order or some other order if there were more data, especially at the very initial moments of the reaction? This is a little unclear. New data may reveal a complex mechanism, such as one where an intermediate directly changes the activation energy profile of the overall reaction, or where a product is an autocatalyst that is immediately present at the start of the reaction. I don't know why you are so confident about 1/[A], even though it is a straight line.
